Share options, restricted stock units (RSUs) and other employee equity awards can add complexity to the financial information considered during divorce and other family financial proceedings.

Unlike ordinary salary, equity compensation may involve vesting conditions, exercise requirements, employer restrictions, tax-related information and future events. The relevant financial records can therefore require more detailed review than a standard payslip or bank statement.

A forensic accountant instructed in a family law matter may be asked to examine these records and explain the financial information relevant to specific questions. The scope of that work depends on the circumstances of the case, the available evidence and the expert's instructions.

## What Are Share Options and RSUs?

Employee equity can take several forms. The financial and contractual characteristics of each award can differ, so identifying the type of award is an important first step.

### Share Options

A share option generally gives an employee the right, subject to its terms, to acquire shares at a specified price or under a specified pricing arrangement.

An option may include conditions relating to:

- Vesting
- Exercise periods
- Continued employment
- Company performance
- Change of control
- Expiry
- Restrictions on transfer or exercise

An option is not necessarily equivalent to owning the underlying shares. Its potential financial value can depend on the particular award terms and circumstances.

### Restricted Stock Units

An RSU is generally a contractual employee award that may result in shares or a cash equivalent being delivered when specified conditions are satisfied.

RSUs often include vesting schedules, although the precise arrangements depend on the relevant employer and plan.

The financial analysis may therefore need to distinguish between:

- Awards that have vested
- Awards that remain unvested
- Awards subject to performance or other conditions
- Shares already received following vesting
- Awards that have been cancelled or forfeited

### Other Employee Equity Awards

Employees may also receive restricted shares, performance share awards, employee share purchase arrangements and other forms of equity compensation.

The terminology used by an employer may not provide the complete financial picture. The underlying plan documents and individual award agreements may need to be reviewed.

## Why Can Equity Awards Require Detailed Financial Analysis?

The existence of an equity award does not, by itself, establish its financial value or determine how it should be treated in family proceedings.

Several questions may need to be considered.

### Has the Award Vested?

Vesting status can be relevant to understanding the employee's rights at a particular date.

An award that has vested may have different characteristics from one that remains subject to future conditions. The relevant records may therefore need to establish the status of each award at the appropriate date.

### Can the Award Be Exercised or Sold?

An award might be vested but still subject to restrictions concerning exercise, transfer or sale.

The relevant plan documents and award agreements can help establish what rights the employee has and when those rights may be exercised.

### What Information Is Available About the Underlying Shares?

For publicly traded shares, market information may be available. However, the relevant valuation date, number of shares and type of interest still need to be established.

For private companies, there may be no readily observable market price. Additional financial and corporate information may therefore be required if a valuation is within the scope of the instruction.

### Are There Tax-Related Records?

Tax treatment can vary depending on the type of equity award, timing, applicable rules and the individual's circumstances.

A forensic accountant may review tax-related records where they are relevant to a financial calculation. Specialist tax advice may be appropriate where the issue requires a separate tax opinion.

## What Documents May Be Relevant?

The documents required will depend on the type of award and the questions the expert has been asked to address.

Potentially relevant records may include:

### Equity Plan Documents

These may explain vesting, exercise, forfeiture, transfer restrictions and other conditions.

### Individual Award Agreements

The individual grant documentation may contain details that are not apparent from the general plan rules.

### Employer Statements

Employer records may show grants, vesting activity, exercises, cancellations and current or historical holdings.

### Payslips and Remuneration Records

Payroll records may help identify equity-related amounts, deductions or other entries associated with an award.

### Tax Records

Tax documentation may provide information about amounts recognised for tax purposes and the timing of relevant transactions.

### Brokerage and Investment Statements

Where shares have been issued or sold through an investment platform, statements may help establish holdings, transactions and proceeds.

### Employment and Compensation Records

Employment agreements, remuneration statements and related records may provide additional information about the nature and timing of equity awards.

## How Might a Forensic Accountant Analyse Equity Awards?

The precise approach depends on the questions raised in the case. A review may involve several stages.

### 1. Identify the Type of Award

The accountant may establish whether the relevant compensation consists of options, RSUs, shares or another form of employee equity.

This distinction can affect the subsequent financial analysis.

### 2. Establish the Relevant Dates

The review may need to identify:

- Grant dates
- Vesting dates
- Exercise dates
- Sale dates
- Expiry dates
- Relevant valuation dates

The significance of each date depends on the particular issue being examined.

### 3. Reconcile the Available Records

The accountant may compare employer records with payslips, tax documentation, brokerage statements and other available financial records.

This can help distinguish between awards granted, awards vested, shares received and shares subsequently sold.

### 4. Consider Available Financial Information

Where a calculation or valuation is required, the accountant may consider the available information about the relevant shares or awards.

For publicly traded shares, market information may be available. For private company interests, additional valuation information may be necessary.

Any calculation should set out relevant assumptions and limitations.

### 5. Consider the Source and Timing of the Award

The circumstances in which an award was granted may be relevant to the questions the expert has been instructed to consider.

For example, an award may relate to employment services performed over a particular period. Establishing the relevant facts may require examination of employment and remuneration records.

This does not automatically determine the treatment of the award. The appropriate analysis depends on the issues in the proceedings and the scope of the expert's instructions.

## Unvested Equity Awards

Unvested awards can present additional questions because the employee may not yet have an unconditional entitlement to the underlying shares.

The available records may need to address:

- Conditions attached to vesting
- The remaining vesting period
- Continued employment requirements
- Performance conditions
- Consequences of leaving employment
- Potential forfeiture
- Transfer restrictions
- Available information about the underlying company or shares

These factors may affect the financial analysis. An unvested award should not automatically be treated in the same way as an unrestricted shareholding.

## Shares Received After Vesting

Once an award has vested and shares have been delivered, the review may need to consider what happened to those shares.

Relevant records may include:

- Number of shares received
- Date of delivery
- Shares sold to meet applicable deductions or tax obligations
- Subsequent sales
- Current holdings
- Sale proceeds
- Related transaction costs

A reconciliation of these records may help establish the movement from the original award to the resulting shareholding or proceeds.

## Equity Awards in Private Companies

Private company equity can involve additional valuation considerations because there may be no publicly quoted market price.

Different share classes can also have different rights. Depending on the circumstances, the analysis may need to consider:

- The class of shares involved
- Voting rights
- Dividend rights
- Transfer restrictions
- Preference rights
- Relevant company financial information
- Recent transactions involving the company
- The terms of the employee's award

If a valuation is required, the appropriate methodology will depend on the available evidence, the nature of the interest and the purpose of the analysis.

## Questions a Family Lawyer May Need to Consider

Where employee equity forms part of the financial picture, a solicitor may need to establish what information is available and what specific question the expert is being asked to address.

Potential questions may include:

### What Awards Does the Individual Hold?

The available records may need to establish grants, vesting events, exercises and current holdings.

### What Remains Unvested?

The expert may need to identify awards that have not yet vested and the conditions attached to them.

### What Shares Have Already Been Received?

This can help distinguish existing shareholdings from future or conditional awards.

### What Financial Calculation Is Required?

If a valuation or other calculation is needed, the relevant date, methodology, assumptions and limitations should be identified.

### What Tax-Related Information Is Relevant?

The accountant may identify tax information relevant to the calculation while distinguishing financial analysis from specialist tax advice.

### What Records Are Missing?

Where the available documentation is insufficient to answer a particular question, the report may need to identify that limitation rather than assume facts that have not been established.

## Preparing the Financial Records for Review

A clear set of records can make it easier to establish the history and current status of employee equity awards.

Depending on the circumstances, relevant records may include:

- Equity plan rules
- Individual grant notices
- Vesting statements
- Exercise records
- Employer remuneration statements
- Payslips
- Tax records
- Brokerage statements
- Records of share sales
- Employment documentation

The existing [financial disclosure guide](/guides/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide) provides broader information about organising and reviewing financial disclosure in divorce proceedings.

## What If the Records Do Not Match?

Differences between employer records, payslips, tax documents and brokerage statements may require further examination.

A discrepancy does not necessarily establish that information has been withheld or misstated. It may result from timing differences, different reporting methods, deductions, cancelled awards or other factors.

A forensic accountant may compare the available records and identify matters requiring clarification. Where information remains unavailable, the limitations can be recorded rather than drawing conclusions that the evidence does not support.

## Conclusion

Share options, RSUs and other employee equity awards can involve financial information that is more complex than ordinary employment income. Their analysis may require consideration of award terms, vesting conditions, relevant dates, share information, transactions and tax-related records.

A forensic accountant may assist by organising and analysing the financial evidence relevant to the specific questions raised in a family proceedings matter. The appropriate approach depends on the type of award, the available evidence and the scope of the expert's instructions.

Where the records do not establish a particular fact, a careful analysis should identify the uncertainty or limitation rather than assume an outcome.
